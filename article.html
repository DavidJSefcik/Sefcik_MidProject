Heywood Goes Dark: One Clinic Kept the Lights On With Salvaged Batteries

Forty-one blocks lost power at 2:14 a.m. A free clinic on Sixth Street never lost a single monitor.

By Mara “Quill” Okafor

March 14, 2045

7 min read

When the Heywood substation failed at 2:14 a.m. last Tuesday, forty-one blocks went dark at once. Neon signs died mid-flicker, elevated trains stalled between stations, and thousands of residents woke to a silence the district had not known since the Time of the Red. Within minutes the streets filled with people holding phones that no longer had a signal.

On Sixth Street, behind a shuttered noodle shop, one building stayed lit. The Sixth Street Free Clinic, run by six volunteers and a rotating cast of off-duty medics, kept every monitor, incubator, and dialysis pump running for the next nineteen hours.

The failure

City engineers blame an overloaded transformer that had been flagged for replacement twice in three years. Records obtained by the Signal show the repair request sat in a queue behind contracts for corporate-zone upgrades. A utility spokesperson called the delay regrettable and declined to say who approved the order of work.

For most residents the outage meant spoiled food and a long walk. For the clinic, it meant forty patients on powered equipment and no margin for error. Dr. Imani Reyes, who has directed the clinic for six years, said the first ten minutes were quiet, then very busy.

Inside, the team triaged by flashlight. Nurses moved the most fragile patients closest to the working outlets, while volunteers hand-pumped ventilation for two infants until the first battery rack arrived. Nobody left their post, Reyes said, and nobody had to be told what to do. The drills they had practiced for years finally felt like something other than paperwork.

The grid let us down, so we built our own.

A Heywood courier

Batteries and backroom deals

The clinic's backup came from an unlikely place: a courier collective that had spent months quietly collecting spent e-bike cells. Riders wired them into racks, tested them on a workbench, and delivered them to the clinic door in under an hour, hand-labeled with their remaining charge.

"Nobody asked us to," said one courier, who asked not to be named because her routes cross three gang territories. "The grid let us down, so we built our own." Nearby vendors donated extension cables, and a neighborhood fixer arranged for a diesel generator to arrive by dawn, no questions asked.

What comes next

Power returned to most blocks by Wednesday evening, though three remain dark as crews replace damaged lines. The utility has promised a review, a phrase residents have heard before. Council members have scheduled a hearing, and the clinic has been invited to testify. Residents plan to attend in force, and several have already organized a ride share so that no one on a night shift misses the chance to speak.

Local business owners are tallying their own losses. A Sixth Street grocer estimates he threw out three thousand eurodollars of dairy and frozen stock, and he is not alone. Several shopkeepers are asking the city for compensation, though few expect to see it. Instead, many have started a shared fund, with each stall chipping in what it can spare.

Reyes says she would rather the clinic never needed the batteries again. Still, the racks will stay on the wall, charged and labeled, a small reminder of how quickly a neighborhood can organize when it has to. "We were lucky," she said. "Luck is not a power plan."
